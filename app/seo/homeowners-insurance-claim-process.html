Understanding the Process

The Homeowners Insurance Claim Process — What to Expect at Every Stage

Most policyholders don't know what happens next. Understanding the process before it unfolds puts you in control of it.

Stage 1 — First Notice of Loss (Days 1–3)

You report the loss to your insurer. They assign a claim number and a field adjuster. You receive a reservation of rights letter in complex claims — this is normal and does not mean your claim is being denied. Your job at this stage is to document everything and submit written notice.

Stage 2 — Investigation and Inspection (Days 3–14)

The adjuster inspects your property and prepares their estimate. This is the most critical stage for documentation. Be present. Walk them through every area of damage. Request their name, contact information, and supervisor's name in writing.

Stage 3 — Estimate and Coverage Determination (Days 14–30)

The insurer issues their estimate and coverage determination. This is where most underpayment occurs. Review the estimate against your independent contractor's pricing before responding. Do not accept it as final.

Stage 4 — Negotiation and Supplement (Days 30–60)

If you dispute the estimate, submit a written supplement request with your contractor's documentation attached. Most claims resolve at this stage when the policyholder submits complete, professional documentation. The insurer issues a revised estimate or a final offer.

Claims that stall at Stage 4 almost always do so because the policyholder did not submit a written supplement with documentation attached. A phone call is not a supplement request. A written letter with an estimate attached is.

Stage 5 — Settlement and Payment (Days 60–90)

You and the insurer agree on a final amount. You receive an ACV payment immediately. If you have RCV coverage, you receive the depreciation holdback after you complete repairs and submit proof. Keep every contractor receipt — you need them to collect the full RCV amount.

When the Process Stalls

Claims stall when documentation is incomplete, when the insurer disputes the cause of loss, or when the gap between estimates is large enough that neither party accepts the other's number. At that point, the appraisal clause provides a formal resolution mechanism outside of litigation.
