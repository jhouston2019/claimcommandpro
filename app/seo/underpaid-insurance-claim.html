How Underpayment Happens

Insurance underpayment is rarely the result of a single mistake. It is the cumulative effect of pricing suppression, scope omissions, improper depreciation, and missing line items — each one individually small, collectively significant. Insurers use proprietary estimating software that systematically prices labor and materials below market rates in most regions.

The Most Common Underpayment Patterns

  • Omitted overhead and profit (O&P) — required on most claims involving a general contractor
  • Labor rate suppression — insurer uses rates 15–30% below local contractor pricing
  • Missing scope items — damage visible in photos not reflected in the estimate
  • Improper depreciation — non-depreciable items depreciated anyway
  • Code upgrade omissions — required building code upgrades not included
  • Matching clause violations — damaged materials replaced only partially when the full surface must match

How to Document the Gap

The most effective way to document underpayment is a line-item comparison: the insurer's estimate on one side, an independent contractor's estimate on the other, with every variance identified and quantified. This document becomes the basis for your supplement request or appraisal demand.

The documented gap is not an opinion. It is the arithmetic difference between what your policy requires the insurer to pay and what they actually paid. That number is disputable.

Your Recovery Options

  • Written supplement request with independent estimate attached
  • Formal demand letter citing policy language and regulatory requirements
  • Appraisal clause invocation (binding, faster than litigation)
  • State insurance department complaint (triggers regulatory review)
  • Bad faith claim (applicable when delay or denial is unreasonable)
