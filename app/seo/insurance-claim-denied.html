Why Claims Get Denied

Insurance claims are denied for four primary reasons: the insurer claims the damage is excluded by the policy, the insurer claims the damage predates the policy or was caused by neglect, the policyholder failed to meet a procedural requirement (late notice, missed proof of loss deadline), or the insurer cannot verify the cause of loss.

The most common wrongful denials involve exclusion misapplication — the insurer cites an exclusion that does not actually apply to the cause of loss when the policy is read carefully.

What to Look for in the Denial Letter

  • The specific policy section cited as the basis for denial
  • Whether the cited exclusion actually applies to your cause of loss
  • Whether the denial references a procedural failure vs. a coverage dispute
  • The deadline to appeal (usually 30–60 days from denial date)
  • Whether the letter is a full denial or a partial denial

How to Build Your Appeal

An effective appeal does three things: it disputes the specific policy language cited in the denial, it provides documentation that contradicts the insurer's factual claims, and it cites your state's claim handling regulations that require the insurer to pay covered losses.

Never respond to a denial with emotion. Respond with the specific policy section the insurer cited, the specific reason their interpretation is incorrect, and the documentation that proves your loss is covered.

When to Escalate

  • File a Department of Insurance complaint if the denial is clearly unreasonable
  • Request a certified copy of your complete claim file
  • Invoke the appraisal clause if the denial is about amount, not coverage
  • Consult a public adjuster or policyholder attorney for complex denials
