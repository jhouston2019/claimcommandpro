What Is Bad Faith Insurance?

When an insurer delays, denies, or underpays your claim without reasonable basis, that is bad faith. Here is what it looks like and what to do about it.

Definition
Insurance bad faith is the failure of an insurer to act honestly, promptly, and fairly in handling a policyholder's claim — including unreasonable delays, improper denials, and failure to pay covered losses.

What Constitutes Bad Faith

  • Unreasonable delay in acknowledging, investigating, or paying a claim
  • Denial without citing specific policy language
  • Misrepresentation of policy provisions
  • Failure to conduct a reasonable investigation
  • Lowball offers with no supporting rationale
  • Repeated requests for the same documentation already submitted
  • Failure to pay undisputed portions of a claim while disputing others

How Bad Faith Differs from a Dispute

Not every denied or disputed claim is bad faith. If the insurer has a reasonable basis for their position — even if you disagree — it is a coverage dispute, not bad faith. Bad faith requires unreasonable conduct — denying without policy support, delaying without justification, or misrepresenting what the policy says.

How to Document Bad Faith

  • Maintain a detailed claim diary with every contact and commitment
  • Keep copies of every letter and document submitted
  • Note every response deadline that was missed
  • Compare insurer conduct to your state's claim handling regulations

Filing a Department of Insurance complaint is the fastest response to bad faith conduct. It is free, creates a regulatory record, and triggers a mandatory insurer response. It is not the same as a bad faith lawsuit — but it often resolves the underlying claim.
