What Is Depreciation in an Insurance Claim?

Depreciation is how insurers pay you less than replacement cost. Here is how the calculation works — and where it is most commonly wrong.

Definition
Depreciation in insurance is a reduction applied to the replacement cost of damaged property to reflect its age and wear — converting replacement cost value to actual cash value.

How Depreciation Is Calculated

Depreciation is calculated based on the item's expected useful life and current age. If a roof has a 20-year expected life and is 10 years old, it has used 50 percent of its useful life. On a $20,000 replacement cost, the depreciated ACV is $10,000. The insurer pays $10,000 initially — and releases the $10,000 holdback after repairs if you have RCV coverage.

What Can and Cannot Be Depreciated

  • Can be depreciated — materials with a measurable useful life (shingles, flooring, appliances, personal property)
  • Cannot be depreciated — labor, permits, disposal fees, and other non-material costs
  • State-specific rules — some states prohibit labor depreciation entirely

Common Depreciation Errors

  • Depreciation applied to labor costs (improper in most jurisdictions)
  • Depreciation applied to permits and disposal (not depreciable)
  • Excessive depreciation percentage based on incorrect useful life
  • Blanket depreciation rate applied instead of item-by-item calculation

Labor depreciation is improper under most state regulations and standard policy language. If your estimate shows depreciation applied to labor line items, that amount is recoverable through a supplement request.
