Dispute Terms

What Is an Insurance Claim Denial?

A denial is not a final answer — it is a legal document that must cite specific policy language. Here is how to read one and whether to appeal it.

Definition
An insurance claim denial is a written determination from an insurer that a specific loss is not covered under the policy — which must cite the specific policy provision on which the denial is based.

What a Valid Denial Requires

  • Written notification to the policyholder
  • Specific policy section or exclusion cited as the basis for denial
  • Explanation of how the cited provision applies to the loss
  • Information about appeal rights

Types of Denials

  • Full denial — entire claim denied
  • Partial denial — some items covered, others denied
  • Coverage denial — insurer says the peril is excluded
  • Procedural denial — late notice, missed proof of loss deadline
  • Valuation dispute — coverage acknowledged but amount disputed

How to Read a Denial Letter

Identify the specific exclusion or policy provision cited. Read that provision in your policy exactly as written. Ask whether the provision actually describes your cause of loss. If it does not, the denial is based on exclusion misapplication and is appealable.

The most common wrongful denial is exclusion misapplication — citing an exclusion that does not accurately describe the cause of loss. Read the cited provision against your actual facts before assuming the denial is valid.
