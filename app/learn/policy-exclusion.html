Coverage Terms

What Is a Policy Exclusion?

Exclusions are how insurers deny claims. Here is how to read them correctly — and how to identify when one does not actually apply to your loss.

Definition
A policy exclusion is a specific loss, cause of loss, or condition that your homeowners insurance policy explicitly does not cover — removing it from the otherwise broad coverage provided by the policy.

How Exclusions Work

In an open perils policy like the HO-3, all causes of loss are covered unless specifically excluded. Exclusions are listed in a dedicated section of your policy. An insurer denying a claim on exclusion grounds must cite the specific exclusion language and explain how it applies to your cause of loss. A vague reference to an exclusion is not a valid denial.

Common Exclusions in Standard Policies

  • Flood — rising water from outside the home
  • Earthquake — ground movement
  • Normal wear and tear — gradual deterioration
  • Intentional acts — damage caused on purpose
  • Neglect — failure to maintain the property
  • Business activities — losses from business use of the home
  • Government action — condemnation or seizure

How Insurers Misapply Exclusions

The most common wrongful denial involves exclusion misapplication — citing an exclusion that does not actually apply to the cause of loss when the policy is read carefully. Common examples include calling sudden storm damage "wear and tear," reclassifying an acute pipe failure as "gradual leakage," or applying the flood exclusion to internal water damage that did not result from rising external water.

When an insurer denies your claim citing an exclusion, request the specific exclusion language in writing. Read it carefully against your actual cause of loss. If the exclusion does not describe what happened to your property, the denial is appealable.
