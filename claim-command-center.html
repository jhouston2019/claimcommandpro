Why This Matters

Most policyholders lose money early in the claim because key steps are missed or handled incorrectly. Understanding what to expect before you act protects your rights and gives you the best chance at a full recovery.

What You Do

The Complete Claim Process: What to Expect

An insurance claim follows a predictable sequence. Understanding this sequence helps you prepare for each stage and avoid common mistakes. Here's what happens from the moment you discover damage until you receive final payment.

Phase 1: Set Up Your Claim (Days 1-14)

Goal: Start your claim correctly so nothing gets limited or denied later.

  • Step 1 — Enter Your Claim Details: Add your claim and policy details so we can analyze your estimate and identify what may be missing.
  • Step 2 — See What Your Policy Covers: Understand exactly what your policy includes — including coverage your insurer may not point out.
  • Step 3 — Set Up Your Claim the Right Way: Make sure your claim is structured correctly from the start.
Phase 2: Document Your Damage (Days 7-45)

Goal: Capture the evidence needed to support full payment.

  • Step 4 — Document All Damage: Capture everything — missed damage means missed money.
  • Step 5 — Get Accurate Repair Estimates: Use real-world pricing so your claim reflects actual repair costs.
  • Step 6 — Prepare for the Adjuster Inspection: Make sure nothing is overlooked or minimized.
  • Step 7 — List Everything You Lost: Account for all contents and items.
Phase 3: Find What's Missing (Days 30-60)

Goal: Identify what's missing, underpaid, or incorrect.

  • Step 8 — Review Your Insurance Estimate: See exactly what your insurer included — and what they didn't.
  • Step 9 — Find Underpaid Items: Identify where pricing is below market.
  • Step 10 — Find Missing Coverage: Uncover policy benefits that were not applied.
Phase 4: Request the Money (Days 45-90)

Goal: Submit corrections and recover the money you're owed.

  • Step 11 — Request Additional Payment: Submit a supplement for missed or underpaid items.
  • Step 12 — Send Claim Letters: Use clear communication your insurer will respond to.
  • Step 13 — Recover Depreciation: Claim withheld funds from your initial payment.
Phase 5: Finish & Get Paid (Days 60-120)

Goal: Finalize your claim at the correct value — not the initial estimate.

  • Step 14 — Negotiate Your Settlement: Push your claim to the correct value.
  • Step 15 — Escalate If Needed: Use appraisal or mediation if required.
  • Step 16 — Finalize Your Claim: Make sure nothing is left unclaimed.
  • Step 17 — Confirm All Payments: Verify every dollar owed has been paid.
  • Step 18 — Close Your Claim: Finish your claim knowing it was handled correctly.

Each step tells you exactly what to do and why it matters — nothing is left to guesswork.

How Insurance Companies Calculate Your Claim

Understanding how insurers calculate payments helps you spot where they're shortchanging you. Here's the process they follow:

  • Step 1 — Determine Coverage: The adjuster reviews your policy to confirm the loss is covered and identifies which coverage sections apply. They look for exclusions or limitations that could reduce or deny your claim.
  • Step 2 — Estimate Repair Costs: The adjuster creates a line-item estimate using pricing software (usually Xactimate). They often use outdated pricing, omit necessary work, and apply aggressive depreciation to reduce the payout.
  • Step 3 — Apply Deductible: Your deductible is subtracted from the total estimate. Make sure they're using the correct deductible amount from your policy declarations page.
  • Step 4 — Calculate Depreciation: For Replacement Cost Value (RCV) policies, they pay Actual Cash Value (ACV) up front and hold back depreciation until repairs are done. Many policyholders never recover this holdback because they don't know how to claim it.
  • Step 5 — Issue Payment: The insurer issues a check for the ACV amount. This is almost always less than what repairs actually cost. You need to identify the gaps and submit a supplement request to recover the difference.
The Most Common Ways Insurers Underpay Claims

Insurance companies use predictable tactics to reduce payouts. Knowing these tactics helps you catch them before you accept a lowball offer.

  • Missing Scope Items: The adjuster "misses" damaged areas or necessary repairs during the inspection. They claim they didn't see it or it wasn't part of the loss. This is why thorough documentation before the inspection is critical.
  • Undervalued Line Items: The estimate includes the work but prices it below market rates. They use outdated pricing databases or apply arbitrary reductions to labor rates and material costs.
  • Aggressive Depreciation: They apply excessive depreciation to materials that don't depreciate that much (like lumber, drywall, or roofing materials). This reduces your ACV payment and the RCV holdback.
  • Denied Coverage Provisions: They fail to apply coverage you're entitled to under your policy — code upgrade coverage, ordinance and law, overhead and profit on large losses, or additional coverages listed in your endorsements.
  • Quantity Manipulation: They reduce quantities of materials needed (square footage of flooring, linear feet of trim, number of outlets) to lower the total cost without it being obvious.
  • Contents Denials: They deny personal property items claiming you didn't prove ownership or the item wasn't damaged. This is why a complete contents inventory with photos and receipts is essential.

Critical Deadlines You Must Know

Missing these deadlines can forfeit your rights permanently. Mark these dates on your calendar as soon as you discover the loss.

  • Notice of Loss: Most policies require you to report the loss "promptly" or within 60 days. Late reporting can result in denial. Report as soon as you discover damage.
  • Proof of Loss: You typically have 60-90 days from the date of loss to submit a sworn Proof of Loss statement. Some policies require this before they'll pay anything.
  • Appraisal Invocation: If your policy has an appraisal clause, you usually have 1-2 years from the date of loss to invoke it. Check your policy for the exact deadline.
  • Lawsuit Filing: Most states have a statute of limitations of 2-5 years for insurance disputes. If you're considering litigation, don't wait until the last minute to consult an attorney.
  • RCV Recovery: You typically have 180-365 days after the ACV payment to complete repairs and claim the depreciation holdback. If you miss this deadline, you forfeit the holdback permanently.
What to Do If You've Already Started Your Claim

If you've already reported your loss or received an estimate from your insurer, don't panic. You can still use this system to improve your position. Here's what to do based on where you are:

  • If you've only reported the loss: Start at Step 2 (Policy Review) and continue through the process. You haven't lost any rights yet.
  • If the adjuster has already inspected: Start at Step 4 (Document Damage) and photograph everything now before repairs begin. Then jump to Step 8 when you receive the estimate.
  • If you've received an estimate: Jump to Step 8 (Review Insurance Estimate) and use our tools to identify gaps. You can still submit a supplement request even if you initially accepted the estimate.
  • If you've already cashed the check: You can still request additional payment if you discover the estimate was incomplete. Start at Step 8 and build your supplement case.
  • If you've signed a release: Review what you signed carefully. Some releases are limited to specific payments and don't prevent future claims. If you signed a broad release, consult an attorney about your options.
Common Mistakes That Cost Policyholders Thousands

Avoid these mistakes that weaken your claim and reduce your payout:

  • Starting repairs before documenting damage: Once you repair or replace something, you can't prove it was damaged. Always photograph first, repair later.
  • Accepting the first offer without review: The insurer's first estimate is almost always incomplete. Never accept it without comparing against contractor estimates and checking for gaps.
  • Signing documents without reading them: Releases, proof of loss statements, and sworn statements can limit your rights. Read everything before you sign and never sign under pressure.
  • Letting the adjuster rush the inspection: If the adjuster tries to speed through the inspection, slow them down. Make them look at every damaged area. What they don't see, they won't pay for.
  • Failing to get contractor estimates: Without contractor estimates, you have no leverage to challenge the insurer's pricing. Get at least two estimates before you negotiate.
  • Not tracking all expenses: Additional living expenses, emergency repairs, and professional fees are all recoverable. Track every dollar you spend related to the loss.
  • Missing the RCV recovery deadline: If you don't claim the depreciation holdback within the policy deadline (usually 180-365 days), you forfeit it permanently. Set a calendar reminder.
Key Insurance Terms You Need to Know

Understanding these terms helps you read your policy and communicate effectively with your insurer:

  • Actual Cash Value (ACV): The depreciated value of damaged property. This is what insurers pay up front on RCV policies. It's the replacement cost minus depreciation.
  • Replacement Cost Value (RCV): The cost to replace damaged property with new materials of like kind and quality. Most policies pay ACV first, then RCV after repairs are complete.
  • Depreciation Holdback: The difference between RCV and ACV. The insurer holds this back until you complete repairs and submit proof. This is often 20-40% of the total claim value.
  • Overhead and Profit (O&P): Contractor markup for managing the job. Typically 10% overhead + 10% profit = 20% total. Required when the loss involves three or more trades (plumber, electrician, carpenter, etc.).
  • Ordinance and Law Coverage: Pays for upgrades required by current building codes. If your roof was built in 1985 and current code requires different materials or methods, this coverage pays the difference.
  • Code Upgrade Coverage: Similar to ordinance and law. Covers the cost difference between repairing to original condition vs upgrading to meet current codes.
  • Additional Living Expenses (ALE): Covers hotel, meals, and other expenses if your home is uninhabitable during repairs. Usually limited to 12-24 months or a percentage of your dwelling coverage.
  • Supplement: A request for additional payment after the initial estimate. Used when you discover the insurer's estimate was incomplete or undervalued.
  • Appraisal: A dispute resolution process in your policy. Each side hires an appraiser, and if they can't agree, an umpire decides. Faster and cheaper than litigation but binding.
  • Public Adjuster (PA): A licensed professional who represents policyholders in claims. They work on contingency (typically 10-20% of settlement). Consider hiring one for complex or high-value claims.
When to Consider Hiring a Professional

Claim Command Pro gives you the tools to handle most claims yourself, but some situations benefit from professional help:

  • Hire a Public Adjuster if: Your claim is over $100,000, involves complex damage (structural, mold, code compliance), the insurer has denied major portions of your claim, or you don't have time to manage the process yourself.
  • Hire an Attorney if: The insurer has acted in bad faith (unreasonable delays, false statements, refusing to investigate), denied your entire claim, or you're considering litigation. Most insurance attorneys work on contingency.
  • Hire an Engineer if: There's a dispute about causation (what caused the damage), structural integrity, or code compliance requirements. Engineers provide expert reports that carry significant weight.
  • Hire a Restoration Company if: You have water damage, mold, fire damage, or any loss requiring emergency mitigation. They document the damage professionally and their reports support your claim.

How Insurance Claims Really Work

An insurance claim is not automatic reimbursement. It's a formal financial review governed by your policy contract. Understanding this process before you act protects your rights and maximizes your recovery.

The 5 Phases of Your Claim

Your claim follows a predictable 5-phase sequence over 60-120 days. Each phase builds on the previous one. Skipping steps or rushing ahead costs you money.

Phase 1: Set Up Your Claim (Days 1-14)

Goal: Start your claim correctly so nothing gets limited or denied later.

  • ✓ Step 1: Understand the claim process
  • ✓ Step 2: Review your policy with AI
  • ✓ Step 3: Report the loss properly

Phase 2: Document Your Damage (Days 7-45)

Goal: Capture the evidence needed to support full payment.

  • ✓ Step 4: Document all damage with photos
  • ✓ Step 5: Get contractor estimates
  • ✓ Step 6: Prepare for adjuster inspection
  • ✓ Step 7: Complete contents inventory

Phase 3: Find What's Missing (Days 30-60)

Goal: Identify what's missing, underpaid, or incorrect.

  • ✓ Step 8: Review insurance estimate with AI
  • ✓ Step 9: Analyze pricing deviations
  • ✓ Step 10: Identify coverage gaps

Phase 4: Request the Money (Days 45-90)

Goal: Submit corrections and recover the money you're owed.

  • ✓ Step 11: Submit supplement request
  • ✓ Step 12: Send dispute letters
  • ✓ Step 13: Recover ACV vs RCV difference

Phase 5: Finish & Get Paid (Days 60-120)

Goal: Finalize your claim at the correct value — not the initial estimate.

  • ✓ Step 14: Negotiate settlement
  • ✓ Step 15: Consider appraisal/mediation
  • ✓ Step 16: Review settlement offer
  • ✓ Step 17: Execute final recovery
  • ✓ Step 18: Close the claim
⚠️ Critical: Don't Skip Steps

Each step builds on the previous one. Skipping documentation (Phase 2) means you can't prove gaps in Phase 3. Skipping analysis (Phase 3) means you don't know what to request in Phase 4. Follow the sequence.
